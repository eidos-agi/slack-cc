⏺ Now I get it.

  This isn't about tracking known competitors doing visible things. This is
  about detecting invisible intent — finding the company that's quietly
  exploring a market before they've filed a single permit, posted a single job,
  or told a single person.

  The signals nobody else is watching:

  - Domain registrations and DNS changes — someone registers "cleanhauldfw.com"
  six months before they show up. SSL certs get provisioned. Subdomains like
  "careers.newwasteco.com" appear.
  - Corporate filings — a new LLC registered in Tarrant County with SIC codes
  matching waste hauling. Secretary of State filings hit days after formation.
  Nobody checks these.
  - Equipment purchases and auction activity — someone buying 12 rear-loaders on
   GovPlanet or Ritchie Bros in the DFW area isn't doing it for fun. CDL truck
  registrations hitting DMV databases.
  - Real estate signals — commercial lease filings, zoning variance requests for
   transfer stations, land purchases near disposal corridors. County assessor
  records are public.
  - Regulatory breadcrumbs — TCEQ permit applications in Texas, IDEM in Indiana.
   Environmental permits take months. The application is the earliest public
  signal that someone is coming.
  - Supply chain signals — container manufacturers, cart distributors, truck
  upfitters seeing new orders from unfamiliar entities in your geography.
  - LinkedIn and hiring patterns — not just job postings. A route manager in
  Indianapolis suddenly connects with 15 people at a company you've never heard
  of. A GM in Memphis updates their title to a new company name. People move
  before companies announce.
  - Loan and bond filings — SBA filings, municipal bond issuances for waste
  infrastructure. UCC filings where waste equipment is collateral for a new
  entity.

  What makes this actually work: None of these signals mean anything alone. A
  domain registration is noise. A zoning request is noise. An LLC filing is
  noise. But the fusion is the product — RRF across all these sources,
  embeddings that understand the semantic relationship between a truck purchase
  in Denton County and a permit application in Denton County from two different
  entity names that share a registered agent. The agents connect dots that no 
  human would ever see because the dots are in 14 different databases that 
  nobody looks at simultaneously.

  The output isn't a report. It's: "There's a 78% probability that a new hauler 
  is entering the Denton County residential market within 6 months. Here's why."
   — delivered before the competitor has even told their own employees.

  This is asymmetric intelligence. Michael and Lannis don't just know who's in
  the market — they know who's coming to the market. That's the difference
  between reacting to a competitor and pre-empting them.

  So the question: Is this about defense (protecting existing routes and
  contracts from stealth entrants), offense (identifying markets where weak or
  distressed competitors signal an acquisition or expansion opportunity), or
  both — and does this extend to detecting when a customer is quietly shopping
  for a new hauler too?
